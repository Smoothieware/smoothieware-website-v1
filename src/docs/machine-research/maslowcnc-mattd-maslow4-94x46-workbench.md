# Matt D.'s Maslow 4 on a 94.5 × 46 in. Workbench Frame

**Machine identity:** Matt D. (`MattD`)'s individual Maslow 4 setup, documented after its first cut in June 2024. The owner used an existing garage workbench and positioned four bolts to define a 94.5 × 46 in. anchor rectangle. This is smaller than a full-size Maslow frame, although one forum participant noted that the long, narrow proportions could constrain the clean region; that participant's estimate is not an owner measurement.

**Novelty check:** On 2026-09-23, the dossier directory was searched for `MattD`, the thread title, the 94.5 × 46 in. anchor dimensions, and the 18 × 24 in. calibration area. No matching individual-machine dossier was found.

## Frame and calibration area

Matt used an existing garage workbench rather than constructing a dedicated large frame. Four bolts on the table defined the anchor rectangle at 94.5 in. wide by 46 in. tall. A 1/4-in. MDF work sheet, about 24 × 41 in., sat over a separate 1/4-in. plywood spoilboard. To keep the sled from travelling beyond the MDF during calibration, the owner chose an 18 × 24 in. calibration area and the 7 × 7 grid option.

The frame dimensions and calibration area are different quantities: 94.5 × 46 in. describes the anchor rectangle, while 18 × 24 in. is the calibration area the owner selected for this first test. Neither is established as the final usable cut area.

A forum maintainer noted that this anchor geometry could produce a narrow clean region, estimating roughly 63 × 13 in., and suggested the owner might want a second, shorter anchor set for a wider clean area. This is another participant's interpretation based on a frame model, not a measured result for Matt's machine. They clarified that the model's colored warning regions represented increasing error rather than hard travel limits.

## First cut and observed result

For a first project, Matt used MakerCAM to prepare a Maslow logo file focused on its “M,” adding an oval profile cut. They reported that the cut worked and that the community forum had helped them get started. The owner was new to CNC at the time and later learned that an up-cut bit likely contributed to fuzzy edges. The post does not give feed rate, spindle speed, cut depth, material thickness, measured dimensions, or a quantified accuracy result.

Matt wrote that the bolts worked for the cut and that cable ends did not climb during that operation. They had extra nuts available as lock nuts in case climbing occurred but did not use them for this test. This is one reported cut on this installation and does not establish that loose cable ends cannot climb in other conditions.
